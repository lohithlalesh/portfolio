What to outsource, and what to keep, in small business marketing

Outsourcing everything means losing your voice. Keeping everything means it never gets done. The answer for most small businesses is in between, and it's more predictable than you'd think.

The principle behind the split

Keep the things only your business can do. Outsource the things that need specialist skill or that punish mistakes. Your knowledge of customers, your tone and your relationships can't be bought in. Setting up conversion tracking or managing an ad budget can, and paying someone who does it every week is usually cheaper than learning it yourself.

Keep these in-house

  • Your knowledge of customers. You hear their questions every day. Write them down and share them. That list is gold for content and ads.
  • Approvals and brand decisions. Someone in the business should sign off on what goes out.
  • Customer conversations. Replying to reviews, DMs and WhatsApp enquiries is best done by people who know the business, with templates if needed.
  • Raw content. Behind-the-scenes photos and phone videos from the team are often more convincing than polished shoots.

Outsource these

  • Technical setup: website, tracking, Google Business Profile and ad accounts.
  • Paid ads management: platforms change constantly, and mistakes cost real money.
  • Design and editing: turning your raw content into consistent, good-looking posts.
  • SEO and content planning: keyword research, page structure and the writing.
  • Reporting: pulling numbers together into one clear monthly view.

What changes by business type

The split shifts depending on how you sell. A few common cases in the UAE:

  • Restaurants and cafés: keep daily stories and specials in-house, because they're timely. Outsource the Google profile, menu pages and ads. See restaurant marketing in Dubai.
  • Clinics: keep patient communication in-house and outsource content that may need checking against advertising rules. See healthcare clinic marketing.
  • Online shops: outsource the store setup, product feeds and ads, but keep customer service and product knowledge close. See e-commerce marketing in the UAE.
  • B2B services: keep founder-led LinkedIn posts in-house, since buyers want to hear from you. Outsource the website, case study writing and any ads.

How to hand over without losing control

Outsourcing goes wrong when the business loses sight of its own accounts and files. It rarely happens on purpose: a freelancer sets up an account quickly to save time, files live in their personal drive, and a year later nobody remembers the login. These four steps keep you in control from day one.

  1. Keep ownership of every account. Add the freelancer as a user. Google Business Profile, Google Analytics and the ad platforms all support adding people with a set access level.
  2. Write a short brand guide: colours, fonts, tone, words to avoid. See how to write a brief.
  3. Agree on a monthly report with one main number.
  4. Store files in a shared folder you own, not on their computer.

A simple weekly rhythm

Monday

You share customer questions and any raw photos or videos from last week.

Midweek

The marketer sends drafts for approval in one batch.

Within 24 hours

One person approves or comments, so nothing waits.

Month end

A short report and a fifteen-minute call on what to change.

The 80/20

Most small businesses get the biggest return from outsourcing the setup and the ads, while keeping the customer insight and raw content in-house.

Signs the split isn't working

If posts sound like they could be from any business, the marketer isn't getting enough raw material or customer insight from you. If approvals take a week, the rhythm breaks and content goes out late or not at all. If you can't say what last month's main number was, the reporting isn't doing its job. Each of these is fixable, usually by adjusting who does what rather than changing provider.
