Can you use AI to write website content? What Google's policies actually say

"Will Google penalise AI content?" is the wrong question. Google cares about whether content is helpful and original, not which tool helped write it. What it does penalise is a specific pattern that AI makes very easy.

Google's stated position

Google set out its view in a February 2023 post on AI-generated content. The core points are short. Appropriate use of AI or automation is not against its guidelines. Using automation, including AI, to generate content with the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings is a violation of its spam policies. And automation has long been used to produce helpful content such as sports scores, weather forecasts and transcripts.

In other words, the test is purpose and value, not method. A page written by hand to game rankings is a problem. A page drafted with AI, then checked and improved by someone who knows the subject, is not.

What Google targets

Google's spam policies describe scaled content abuse as generating many pages primarily to manipulate search rankings rather than help users, typically large amounts of unoriginal content with little value, "no matter how it's created". Using generative AI to churn out pages without adding value is one of the examples it gives.

This policy arrived in March 2024, alongside two others aimed at similar shortcuts: expired domain abuse (buying an old domain to host low-value content on its reputation) and site reputation abuse (third-party pages hosted on a trusted site to borrow its rankings). When Google takes manual action against a site for spam, the owner is notified in Search Console.

What it looks like in practice

In the UAE, I see the risky pattern most often in three forms:

  • Area pages: the same service page copied for every Dubai community with only the area name changed. This overlaps with Google's doorway abuse policy too. See multi-location SEO.
  • Machine-translated Arabic versions of every page, published without review. See Arabic SEO.
  • Blogs filled with generic AI articles on broad topics that have nothing to do with what the business knows or sells.

What Google rewards

Google's guidance on helpful content asks whether content shows experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness (E-E-A-T), and says trust is the most important. It also says E-E-A-T itself isn't a specific ranking factor, but that its systems use a mix of factors to identify content that demonstrates these qualities.

Bylines and disclosure

Google's FAQ on AI content addresses two practical questions. On bylines, it suggests accurate author bylines wherever readers would reasonably expect them, meaning anywhere someone might wonder "Who wrote this?". It also says that giving AI an author byline is probably not the best approach. On disclosure, it suggests AI or automation disclosures are useful where someone might wonder "How was this created?", and to add them where that would reasonably be expected.

How I use AI tools

I use AI tools every day, and I'm open about it. The rule I follow is that the tool can speed up the work but cannot be responsible for it.

  • For research, outlines, first drafts and checking structure.
  • Never as the final word on facts. Rules, numbers and legal points are checked against official or reputable sources, and those sources are listed.
  • Every piece answers one specific question, rather than repeating a template across topics.
  • A named person (me) reviews and stands behind everything published.

Google's own test

Google suggests asking "Who, How, and Why" about your content: who created it, how it was produced, and why it exists. If the honest answer to "why" is "to rank", that's the problem.

Anyone offering "hundreds of SEO articles a month" for a small fee is describing exactly the pattern Google's policy targets. That is one of the red flags when hiring a marketer.
