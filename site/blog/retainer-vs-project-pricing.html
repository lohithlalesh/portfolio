Retainer or project fee? How to pay a marketing freelancer

The way you pay a marketer shapes the work you get. Retainers reward ongoing attention. Project fees reward finishing. Here's how to pick, and how to avoid paying for a retainer nobody uses.

The difference in one line

A project fee pays for a defined outcome, such as a website, a launch or an audit. A retainer pays for ongoing capacity, such as monthly content, ads management or SEO. Hourly billing exists too, but for small businesses it makes budgeting hard and rewards slow work, so I'd only use it for small, unpredictable jobs.

Why the payment model changes the work

Incentives matter, even with honest people. On a project fee, the marketer is rewarded for finishing well and on time, so scope creep is the main risk: small extra requests pile up until the project never ends. On a retainer, the marketer is rewarded for keeping you as a client, which is good for continuity but can drift into "activity" if nobody checks whether the work still serves the goal.

Neither is better in general. The question is which risk you'd rather manage. For work with an obvious finish line, I'd always choose a project fee. For work that compounds over time, like ads that get better with each month of data, a retainer makes more sense.

Making a retainer work

Most retainer problems come from a vague scope. If neither side can say what a month includes, nobody can tell whether it was delivered.

  • Define the monthly scope in units: number of posts, campaigns managed, articles written. "General marketing support" is how retainers go wrong.
  • Agree on what happens to unused scope. Does it roll over, or is it lost?
  • Review every month against the goal, not just the task list.
  • Keep a short notice period, such as 30 days, so either side can exit cleanly.
  • Revisit the scope every quarter. What the business needed in January may not be what it needs in June.

Making a project fee work

With a project, the details you agree before starting decide how smoothly it ends. Write them down, even if the project is small.

  • Write down what's included, and what's not, before work starts.
  • Agree on the number of revision rounds.
  • Pay in stages for larger projects: a deposit, a payment at a milestone, and the balance on delivery.
  • Agree how extra requests will be handled: quoted separately, or added to a later phase.
  • Decide who owns the files and accounts at the end. The answer should be you.

The hybrid most small businesses end up with

In practice, many Dubai businesses use both. A project fee covers the setup, then a smaller retainer covers the ongoing work that the setup makes possible.

Mistakes to avoid

Side by side

Common traps

Retainer traps

  • Scope written as hours, not deliverables
  • Long lock-in before you know the person
  • No monthly review against a goal
  • Ad spend bundled into the fee

Project traps

  • No written list of what is excluded
  • Unlimited revisions
  • Full payment upfront for a large project
  • Files and logins left with the provider
