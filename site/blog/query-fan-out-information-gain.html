Query fan-out and information gain: why crowded SERPs are not the game

Most SEO advice still starts with "find a keyword with volume and write a better version of what ranks". Google's AI search works differently. It splits a question into many smaller searches and assembles an answer from the pages that add something. That changes what is worth writing.

What query fan-out is

Google described the technique when it announced AI Mode at Google I/O on 20 May 2025: "AI Mode uses our query fan-out technique, breaking down your question into subtopics and issuing a multitude of queries simultaneously on your behalf." Its documentation for site owners says both AI Overviews and AI Mode may use query fan-out, "issuing multiple related searches across subtopics and data sources" to develop a response.

In plain terms: one question from a user becomes many searches behind the scenes. The answer is then built from the pages that best answer each piece, and Google says this lets it show a wider set of supporting links than a classic search.

That table is my illustration of the idea, not a list Google published. The point is that each sub-search is narrow, and a page that answers one of them precisely has a better chance of being used than a page that vaguely covers everything.

What information gain means

Information gain is the idea that a page is more useful if it adds something the reader has not already seen. Google holds a patent on it: US 11,354,342, "Contextual estimation of link information gain", filed in 2018 and granted on 7 June 2022, with inventors Victor Carbune and Pedro Gonnet Anders. It describes giving documents an information gain score based on how much new information they add beyond documents the user has already viewed, and using that score to choose or rank what to show next, including through an automated assistant.

Be careful with how this gets quoted. A patent shows what Google has explored, not what it uses in ranking today. Google has not confirmed an "information gain score" as a ranking factor, and anyone telling you it has is overclaiming. What Google has said publicly points the same way, though. Its helpful content guidance asks: "Does the content provide original information, reporting, research, or analysis?" and whether it offers "interesting information that is beyond the obvious". Its May 2025 advice on AI search says to focus on "unique, non-commodity content".

Why crowded SERPs are not the game

"If there's a topic where Google already has pages ranking and you're in that crowded spot, regardless of search volume, that's not the real game. You have to build your own content." Lohith Lalesh

Here is the reasoning. If ten pages already say the same thing, an eleventh page saying it slightly better adds almost nothing. A fan-out answer only needs one source for each fact, and when the fact is common knowledge, there is little reason to credit you for it. Search volume does not change that. A big keyword where you add nothing new is still a keyword where you add nothing new.

The opening is in the questions where the existing pages are generic and you have something specific: a local price, a real test, a pattern in your own enquiries, a clear opinion from someone who does the work. I still do keyword research, but I use it to find the questions, then ask what I can add that the current results do not have.

How to produce original data cheaply

You do not need a research budget. Most small businesses already sit on useful data and never publish it. The rule is that every number you publish must be real, with its source, sample and date stated.

Five low-cost ways to create original material

Poll your customers

Ask one or two clear questions through WhatsApp, email or Stories. Publish the question wording, how many people answered and when. Small samples are fine if you say how small.

Mine your own enquiries

Count what people ask about most, which areas they come from, which months are busiest. Your CRM, inbox or WhatsApp labels are a dataset.

Run before and after tests

Change one thing (a photo, a page, a process), note the date, and record what happened. Report it honestly, including when nothing changed.

Quote the person who does the work

Ten minutes of recorded conversation with your most experienced team member usually produces three or four quotable opinions.

Document your method

Write down how you do the job, step by step, with real photos. A method is original by definition.

Do not fake it

Invented statistics and made-up case studies are worse than having none. They break trust with readers, and an AI answer that repeats them puts your name next to something false. If you cannot measure it, describe your method instead.

Structuring pages so sub-queries find the answer

Original material only helps if it is easy to find on the page. Fan-out searches are narrow, so structure each page so that a narrow question lands on a clear answer.

  • Use headings that match the sub-questions people ask: price, time, area, process, who it is for.
  • Answer in the first sentence under each heading, then explain. The answer should make sense if quoted alone.
  • Put data in tables or short lists with units, dates and sample sizes in text, not only in images.
  • Name the source of every number, including "our own enquiries, January to June".
  • Give each distinct topic its own page rather than one long page covering everything.
  • Show who wrote it and why they know, with a real byline and short bio.
